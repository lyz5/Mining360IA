1.0 Introduction  
 
Last Labor to Invoice (LLI) is a measure of the time in days from the completion of a repair to 
when the work order is closed and the customer is invoiced.  Managing and working to reduce 
this metric is a critical part of maintaining an efficient service shop or CRC.  Excessive LLI 
delays the timing of cash flows, which can negatively impact a company’s financial position.  In 
some cases, capital charges are levied against Service Work In Process (SWIP) that has yet to 
be invoiced.  Eliminating these non-value added charges will lead to improved profitability.  
Finally, by reducing the time a customer has to wait to be invoiced, increased customer 
satisfaction can be achieved. 
 
Perhaps the most critical element is driving the awareness of the issue across the service 
department.  There are improvements that can be made to the process, but without the 
appreciation of the issue, delays can continue to occur when attention is diverted to what is 
deemed a more important use of time.  This Best Practice documents the work of several 
dealers to determine the root causes and potential solutions to excessive LLI.   
 
A good benchmarks for LLI is three days.  The appropriate goal will vary from dealer to dealer 
based on factors surrounding obtaining customer purchase order numbers, parts/core return 
processes, report reviews, and many others.

2.0 Best Practice Description 
 
It is important to identify the “As-Is” process prior to attempting to make improvements so 
bottlenecks and other opportunities can be identified and then demonstrated through the “To-Be” 
process.  When all details of the process can be viewed, it is much more likely that problems will 
be more apparent and give you a baseline for future improvements.  The LLI process varies from 
dealer to dealer, but the following is an example of an improved process implemented by a 
dealer. 
 
 
 
Many LLI processes allow excessive time during the routing and review portions without giving 
an individual responsibility for ensuring steps are completed in a timely manner.   
 
In the process above, the floor supervisor is responsible for checking over the job upon being 
tagged as “Ready to Go” by the lead hand and then routing the work order to the biller.  Prior to 
routing to the biller, a Work Order Completion checklist is completed.  This can be completed by 
the technician or a supervisor, but should be done by whoever is desired to have responsibility 
for ensuring the work order is complete and is received by the biller in a timely fashion.  The 
checklist ensures that all information necessary to close the work order and invoice the customer 
has been completed.

Upon totaling the work order, if a job is determined to have excessive variance or is a high dollar 
warranty or goodwill job, the biller routes it to a supervisor with a pull system.  This keeps the 
supervisor focused only on work orders that need attention.  Review time can be a major cause 
of delays in processing work orders when they sit unnoticed, adding unnecessary time to the 
process. To combat this delay, excessive variance or high dollar warranty and goodwill work 
orders are required to be reviewed by supervisors on a daily basis to keep the review time to a 
minimum.   
 
Ensuring that routing and reviewing of work orders is completed in a timely manner is only the 
beginning of the potential ways to keep LLI to a minimum.  Other key behaviors include: 
 
• Ensure accurate labor, parts, and miscellaneous ch arges in work orders to minimize back 
outs 
• Turn in complete  service reports the first  time 
• Stay ahead of warranty/goodwill issues 
• Utilize DBS Parts Shop Return process to simplify parts return process (See References ) 
• Develop reliable process for obtaining customer P. O. numbers

There are many potential process improvements, and the deficiency causing the most delay in 
the process can vary from dealer to dealer or even between dealer branch stores.  It is important 
to analyze the situation from all angles to ensure that all the potential root causes are found.  
However, perhaps the most critical step is developing a culture that avoids unnecessary 
delays in the process .  Simply taking steps to ensure the Service Reports to review do not get 
filed under a stack of other work or continuing to follow up until the necessary information is 
gathered will help reduce LLI. 
 
 
3.0 Implementation Steps 
 
The first step to improving LLI is to create an “As-Is” process map.  Once this is complete, 
identify potential root causes with methods such as a fishbone diagram.  An improved process 
designed to address the identified root causes should then be implemented.  An important part 
of the implementation process is providing training.  Without an understanding of the importance 
of LLI across the organization, it will be difficult to maintain improvements in the long-term.  
Some general training that is key to reducing LLI includes: 
  
• Service Warranty Policy 
• Goodwill Policy 
• Parts Warranty Policy 
• Service Report Writing 
• STW – Time and Daily Work Order Submittal  
• Customer Communication and Follow-Up 
• Importance of Reducing LLI 
 
The key to a successful implementation is embedding control methods to ensure that any 
improvements made to the process are followed in the long-term, rather than just falling back 
into the previous, unsuccessful process.  Potential control methods include: 
 
• Weekly DBS reports tracking LLI 
• Track work orders by responsible technician 
• Daily reports visible to Supervisors 
• Develop standard operating procedures 
 
There have been instances in which a dealer showed significant improvement, but without a 
control method in place, fell back to previous levels of performance.  In some cases, the 
performance was even worse than the original state.  No improvement project is complete 
without a control in place.

4.0 Benefits 
Dealers who implemented similar process improvements to those outlined in this Best Practices 
have seen results and benefits such as: 
  
• Reduce LLI by upwards of 50% 
• Improved timing of cash flows leads to reduced int erest expense 
• Improved profitability (If capital charges are lev ied) 
• Overall more efficient service operation 
• Timely invoicing leads to increased customer satis faction 
 
5.0 Resources Required 
 
Parts Shop Return Process Detail 
 
A tool that often goes unused in DBS is the Parts Shop Return Process. It helps to simplify the 
parts return process by allowing the technician to simply choose which parts to return from the list 
associated with the work order in question.  This eliminates additional work looking up the part 
number or group number again and makes it easier to get the correct number the first time.  This 
also raises a flag to the parts department so they know a returned part is in transit to eliminate 
unnecessary backorders.   
 
Ensure that this tool is being used – in some cases, it was not even installed in the dealer’s DBS 
environment.  See the screenshots below to identify if it is installed.  To search for a work order, 
simply enter the work order number or segment number.  If desired, a particular part can also be 
searched for within a work order.
