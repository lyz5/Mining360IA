1.0 Introduction  
Overcooled engines can lead to sludge formation in the crankcase. Sludge can gum up valve 
lifters and stems, as well as piston and piston ring. It can also cause corrosive wear and carbon 
buildup. Catastrophic failure could occur if these conditions went unnoticed.  
 
The most common way engine overcooling can occur is for one or more of the jacket water 
thermostats to be stuck open. Other possible causes of engine overcooling are hydraulic fans 
remaining locked in the fully engaged condition, damage to the vent valve in the thermostat 
housing and extreme ambient temperatures.  
 
A thermostat can become stuck open as a result of a cooling system that has been poorly 
maintained, through the use of raw water or inadequate levels of inhibitor/antifreeze. Raw water 
or poorly maintained coolant will allow the cast iron cylinder block to oxidize and deposit small 
iron particles into the coolant. These iron particles may lodge into the moving parts of the 
thermostats and cause them to stick open.  
 
The recommended interval for thermostats to be changed is every 6,000 hours. However, there is 
a chance that a thermostat would fail between change-out intervals. Hence the situation must be 
frequently monitored in order to prevent catastrophic failure.  
 
2.0 Best Practice Description 
 
This Best Practice describes how to use VIMS to identify an overcooled engine. Plotting a 
histogram of coolant temperature is a great way to identify whether the truck is overcooled but the 
sample size must be big enough to get an accurate diagnosis. Additionally, datalogger can also 
assist in determining if the engine is overcooled.  
 
Stuck open regulator 
 
An engine temperature histogram can be used to identify overcooled engines. A normal histogram 
will show a tight temperature range with the majority falling within the target temperature range.

that 84% of samples have the temperature remaining between 80

Figure 2.2 shows the histogram of a 785B truck with irregular engine coolant temperature. There

below 80

machine with any more than 20% samples below the thermostat’s rating should be investigated 
for incorrect operation.

Sample Count for Histogram 
Sample size is critical in the diagnosis process. If the sample size consists of the half a day of 
data, there is a possibility the truck spent most of its time idling. Data therefore won’t present an 
accurate picture of machine health. A good sample size should include a large portion of time in 
normal operation. Usually, a week of data should give an accurate indication of whether the truck 
is overcooled or operating normally 
 
Damaged Vent Valve in Thermostat Housing 
 
Another possible cause for low coolant temperature is a damaged check valve in the thermostat 
housing. A damaged valve can cause an alternate path for coolant to transfer back to the 
radiator. This coolant transfer is more prevalent in extremely cold conditions and can cause low 
coolant temperature.  Operation in hot environments typically do not show significant effect on 
coolant temperatures when the vent valve is damaged.  
 
When replacing thermostats the check valve should also be replaced to insure it is working 
properly.  
 
Locked up Hydraulic Fan 
 
Engine fan speed and coolant temperature can be used together to determine if the engine fan 
control and cooling circuit are working properly and not over cooling the engine. If the steering 
cycle blips can be seen in the engine fan speed graph or the engine coolant temperature drops 
below 88
oC (190oF), the fan has likely remained fully engaged and is over cooling the engine.

Extreme Ambient Air Temperature 
 
If an application has significant periods of idle time and the ambient temperature is well below 0oC 
(32oF), even with a correctly operating thermostat, the engine jacket water temperature may drop 
below 79oC (175oF). Given enough time at cold enough temperature, the engine jacket water 
temperature may drop to 59oC (130oF) or lower. In these cases, fan clutches should be used to 
help keep the jacket water temperatures at or above 79oC (175oF). To minimize corrosive wear, 
Engine oil must be changed so the oil’s TBN does not drop below 50% of new oil TBN (as per 
ASTM D2896). If the oil TBN gets too low it will not neutralize the acids that form during 
combustion. If these acids are not neutralized, corrosive wear will occur.  
 
3.0 Implementation Steps 
• Develop a process to regularly monitor engine temperature using VIMS data  
• Assign personnel to monitor and track engine coolant temperatures, especially during cold 
seasons  
• If low engine temperature is detected, the steps listed in this Best Practice should be used to 
identify and fix the problem 
  
4.0 Benefits 
The main benefits identified are:  
• Prevent engine overcooling that might lead to catastrophic failure 
• Reduced time in problem diagnosis 
• Increased availability due to early diagnosis and prevention
