MINING DEALER BEST PRACTICE  
 
1.0 Introduction  
 
One of the most important focus areas for mine site operations teams is to maximize haul truck 
“Traveling Loaded Time” while minimizing inefficient time spent idling and stopped loaded.  Not only 
is this time not spent producing, the unnecessary fuel consumption is costly. 
 
Pit depth, haul distance, ore type, and other variables will govern the percentages that a mining haul 
truck would spend in each segment of the haul cycle.  Flatter or downhill hauls would alter the % time 
spent traveling loaded or unloaded.  Length of haul and price of ore might increase the loading % 
time as shovels put half loaded buckets on trucks in order to hit target payload on long hauls.  With 
some variation for the above factors, below would be an example of an acceptable breakdown by 
haul segment type per industry norms.   
 
Example of Haul Cycle Segments 
 
 
As mines focus more and more on reducing fuel consumption for both cost and environmental 
purposes, we are forced to optimize our haul cycles and cut out any waste.  Excessive idling is an 
excellent place to start. 
 
Excessive idling is often times merely an operational inefficiency.  Trucks are left idling during lunch 
breaks, mid-shift breaks, refueling, blasting, shift change, and other necessary mine activities.  There 
can also be environmental reasons.  Those working in extreme climates are most familiar with 
excessive idling often times corresponding to seasons.  During the winter time, trucks are left idling 
longer so that engines don’t require a cold restart and cabs stay warm.  Conversely, in the warm 
summer months, trucks stand idling so that air conditioners can be left running.  While these 
problems are real, more practical, cost effective measures can be taken to keep operators 
comfortable.  
 
Properly identifying, tracking, and putting in place a system to limit machine idling has been elusive 
on many sites.  VIMS data and MineStar Health reporting offers an effective solution for decreasing 
idling and site fuel consumption.

Given the above, the importance of following proper shutdown and start-up procedures cannot be 
stressed enough.  Shutting down the machine while the engine is still hot, not allowing it sufficient 
time to cool can lead catastrophic failure or reduced life.  Likewise, cold engine starts without the use 
of engine preheating can also lead to reduced engine life.  Incremental fuel savings should not be 
substituted for reduced engine life.  Proper procedures from machine OMMs and industry best 
practice should be followed relative to machine shutdown and start-up procedures.  There may be 
good common sense to allowing machines to idle in certain conditions and situations.  The objective 
of this Best Practice is to limit inefficient, lengthy, and unnecessary idling when it occurs. 
 
 
2.0 Best Practice Description 
 
Using VIMS data and MineStar Health software, the user will be able to trend as much historical data 
that is available, separating out fleet idling time as a percentage of total operating time.  If desired, 
this can then be broken down further: 
 
• Seasonally 
• By Shift 
• By Crew 
• By Operator 
 
Once fleet idle times have been tracked and any excessive areas (certain crews or operators, for 
example) have been identified, site can set about eliminating abusive idling.  Strategies will vary by 
site and climate.  Solutions to excessive idling could include one or a combination of the following: 
 
• Employing the automatic shutdown timer switch or lowering the time to shutdown setting. 
• The mine changing operator Standard Work Procedures (SWPs) to include maximum idling 
time. 
• Utilizing pit supervisors to enforce obligatory engine shutdowns during long truck stop periods.  
 
 
3.0 Implementation Steps 
 
 
To implement this download the report (available via the MineStar Reporting KN site). 
 
1. Open the report in Web Intelligence and modify the object _FuelCost to give the correct 
price of fuel for the site.  
2. Ensure that the correct machines are assigned to the correct Fleet. This is done in 
MineStar Client>Machine Finder 
3. Run the report over a period of at least 6 months and save as PDF. 
 
For any questions, please contact MineStar Support

4.0 Benefits 
 
Whether the reasons are purely operational inefficiency, environmental, or a combination of both, 
giving the operations department access to this information empowers them to: 
 
• Decrease fuel consumption by controlling machine operators idling times 
• Optimize their fleet if the idling is a result of being over-trucked 
• Increase production by reallocating trucks that may be underutilized. 
 
 
 
As the below example shows, this mine saw increased idling and fuel consumption during the cooler 
months as operators kept trucks running to avoid cold starts and to keep the cab warm.  Idling as a 
percentage of total operating time was still too high when they reached the summer months.  This we 
were able to trend and benchmark against during the following year.

We had similar findings on another site.  Here an evenly split fleet of 50 x793Ds and 785Cs were 
found to be idling an average of 27% over an 18 month period.   
 
 
 
 
       
MineStar report showing average fleet idle time as a percentage of total operating time 
 
Using Health reporting, we can show the customer the cost of idling over an accepted amount.  With 
a target of 12%, this site was spending roughly $138,000 and $304,000 on excessive idling for its 
785C and 793C fleets, respectively. 
 
 
 
 
 
It is also possible to break down idle time even further.  With the use of Minestar Fleet or another fleet 
management software, we can track idle times by: 
 
• I dle time after machine starts 
• Idle time at shovel or loading tool waiting to load 
• Stopped and idling during haul to dump (intersection, weigh scales, etc.) 
• Idling at the dump 
• Idling during break and shift change (no operator in the cab)

MINING DEALER BEST PRACTICE  
 
 
Customers and dealers can most effectively take action to limit excessive idle fuel burn firstly by 
tracking and documenting of % idle time.  Areas where the inefficiencies are most apparent (shift 
change, the dump, operational downtime like blasting, etc.) must be reviewed and a policy 
implemented to limit idling in these areas.  Local conditions, specifically ambient temperatures, must 
be taken into account.  Site must then reexamine idle times once new idling policies have been 
implemented to gauge improvements.   
 
While a certain level of idling is unavoidable, between 5%-15% depending on site conditions, 
customer, dealer, and Caterpillar teams can focus on decreasing idling above these industry 
benchmarks.  By showing the % fuel consumption above these norms, and the financial impact of this 
excessive idling, we can demonstrate the value of tracking and acting on data gathered through VIMS 
and MineStar.
