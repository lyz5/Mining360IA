1.0 Introduction  
 
• Increasing equipment availability is the objective of all maintenance organizations. A 
robust and fact-based process to identify and address the reasons of unavailability is the 
most effective way to manage and sustainably increase availability.  
• Some customers perform this analysis internally but others lack the resources or 
knowledge and Site Performance Managers from the Dealer and/or Caterpillar can support 
this process, creating a unique relationship with the customers’ maintenance organization. 
• This best practice describes methods to codify and analyze downtime, the process to 
analyze, how to identify opportunities, how to structure a customer meeting to review, and 
how to track action items. 
  
 
2.0 Best Practice Description 
 
 
2.1 Source of Data 
• The source of downtime information can be dispatch data, maintenance records, or a 
combination of both. 
o Ideally, dispatch and Maintenance & Repair records are reconciled on a regular 
basis, therefore downtime categories are accurate in both systems. 
• The quality & accuracy of the data used can substantially influence the analysis results. 
Measuring with sufficient granularity (detail) is important for the data to be meaningful. 
• Quality of initial source data should not delay beginning the analysis because observations 
and improvements are possible with even poor-quality information. 
• Processing data can be done in excel since most or all computerized maintenance 
management software (CMMS) have export functionality.  Some dispatch or maintenance 
software programs have pre-programmed functionalities. 
 
 
2.2 Time Usage Model  
 
To successfully calculate the top tier and M&R process metrics, it is imperative that the mine site 
departments (production operations and maintenance) have a common understanding of the time 
usage model. Teams need to be aligned to ensure a common understanding of categories, terms, 
and calculations. 
 
Caterpillar suggests the “time usage model” as shown in the table below. Customers may have 
different definitions or variations of the time usage model.  When Caterpillar and dealers work with 
any site, it is advisable to ensure the time usage model and coding system are clearly understood.

2.3 Codification of Downtime 
 
Each time a machine stops for maintenance or repair, a few critical things must be captured.  It’s 
preferable to capture any delay time, if applicable.  Critical information for each stoppage should 
include:  
• Stoppage type:  Scheduled or Unscheduled 
• Cause of the stoppage:  PM, PCR, etc. 
• Responsibility code:  Maintenance, Operations, Other, etc.  
• Responsible party:  Customer, Dealer, Tire contractor, Fire suppression contractor, etc. 
• Machine system or subsystem affected (engine, final drives, steering, etc.).   
 
Repair delays cause the repair time to increase beyond the mine standards.  Examples of delays 
might be due to “waiting for parts”, “waiting for labor”, or “waiting for facilities”, etc.  There are two 
primary methods to capture repair delays and either can assist the site to define continuous 
improvement opportunities. 
• Capture all downtime at the machine system or subsystem level then record additional 
second level detail that defines the repair delays. 
• Capture delay codes such as “waiting for parts”, “waiting for labor”, “waiting for facilities”, 
etc. as primary downtime categories. 
 
In this Best Practice example, the mine site uses AMT software, but similar structure can be used for 
any maintenance database tool (SAP, Wenco, JD Edwards). Fields of the database are presented in 
an Access table below.

efficiently performed with preselected categories or systems.  SMCS codes can be used for

2.3.2 Non-Mechanical Causes and Delays 
 
• It is important to capture repair delays because defining these delays provides 
opportunities to target areas for improvement (possible inputs to the Continuous 
Improvement (CI) process).  
o Measuring & reporting delay categories (“waiting for xx”) enables the team to define 
how much “pain” was caused and help motivate changes or improvements in the 
M&R organization (such as additional people, more or different types of tools, more 
parts, etc.). 
• A percentage of the equipment downtime is not due to the repair itself, but delays due to 
waiting for parts, labor, tooling or other resources. 
• A downtime analysis which does not include delays is incomplete and can lead to false 
conclusions. 
• Waiting for parts, waiting for labor, etc. can be documented either in a separate entry, or 
as a variation on the repair (% of time, or hours).

2.3.3 Handling Accidents and Equipment Abuse 
 
• Sometimes the root cause of a component and machine failure is due to operational abuse. 
• A downtime description, such as “accident”, or “operational abuse” needs to be utilized to 
differentiate those and allocate to the appropriate responsibility within the organization. 
 
 
2.3.4 Handling Simultaneous Work and Backlog Performed at PM 
 
• When jobs (work orders) are performed in parallel, downtime should be measured once.  
However, it is still expected that labor hours are accurately captured for each work order in the 
work order management system. 
• Backlogs performed during PM within the PM “window of opportunity” or within a single shift 
can be allocated to PM downtime.  Alternatively, downtime due to PM and downtime due to 
backlog repairs can be captured separately and allocated to the appropriate machine system.  
o It should be reinforced that assigning backlogs within the PM window of opportunity 
is acceptable only if the PM time is reasonable.  Typically, most PM stoppages are 
targeted for completion within a single shift of slightly longer.

2.3.5 Handling Downtime from PM Execution 
 
• Measure PM execution and compare with site goals and industry benchmarks. 
• Often backlogs or repairs downtime is incorrectly allocated to PM, causing the downtime due 
to “PM” to be overinflated and downtime due to other reasons to be underreported in other 
machine systems (such as hydraulics, electrical, etc.).   
o Attention is needed to ensure this doesn’t drive the wrong conclusions. 
o As an example, if the top downtime drivers and impact on availability are used to 
prioritize improvement actions, then overreporting PM and underreporting other work 
could drive the wrong behavior.  
• Trending of “PM unavailability” for trucks is typically more stable and consistent over time due 
to the size of fleets compared to fleets of a single unit or small fleets.  
• When the 8-Step PM process is used, PM trending can be used by grouping all PM types 
together (PM1, PM2, PM3, PM4, PM5, etc.) for each machine equipment model. 
• If the 8-Step PM process is not used, then each machine model and PM type should be 
separated and reported as shown in the table below (i.e. PM250, PM500, PM1000, etc.).

2.5 Identifying & Addressing Root Causes 
 
• Technical reports and feedback from customer and dealer personnel will be required to 
understand and evaluate root cause of failures and other stoppages. 
• At a high-level view, failures might be attributable to maintenance performance, operations 
conditions or behaviors, manufacturer machine or parts quality, or combination these. 
• Continuous Improvement projects are often valuable opportunities to identify and address root 
cause of downtime.

2.6 Availability and Availability Index  
 
Caterpillar recommends using Availability index with physical availability. 
• Physical Availability = (Calendar Hours - Downtime Hours) / Calendar Hours.  
• Availability index = MTBS / (MTBS + MTTR) 
• MTBS = Operating Hours / Number of Shutdowns 
• MTTR = Downtime Hours / Number of Shutdowns 
 
Low utilization (i.e., high standby hours) will artificially inflate Physical Availability (PA) and can 
provide the maintenance department with a false sense of "good performance". 
 
The table below is an example that demonstrates this concept.  In this example, 3 trucks have 
completely different reliability (MTBS), but because of differences in utilization, the physical 
availability results are the same. Therefore, the conclusion is that physical availability without 
understanding utilization can be deceiving.  Availability index is a reporting metric that removes the 
differences due to changes in utilization.  Availability index is a more objective way to measure the 
efficiency (speed) and effectiveness (quality) of the maintenance organization. 
 
 
 
 
2.7 Follow up and Tracking Actions  
 
An action plan is a critical part of implementing improvements.  A 3W (Who, What, When) type 
management methodology is the recommended approach to improving availability. 
 
It’s recommended to conduct monthly meetings with the customer, dealer and Cat representative if 
possible.  This team should include key members of the maintenance organization such as: site 
maintenance manager, senior planner, foreman, condition monitoring, reliability engineering, 
continuous improvement, and the Cat & dealer site performance managers.

3.0 Implementation Steps 
 
The first step is to get agreement or alignment with the customer, Dealer and Caterpillar personnel 
about the objectives of the exercise, roles & responsibilities, and resources.  Customers often 
have apprehension about sharing internal information so developing a trusting relationship is 
necessary prior to this stage. Agreement and commitment of all parties (customer, dealer, 
Caterpillar) at a management level helps get traction and start the process in the right way. 
 
An important reminder is that no data is perfectly accurate.  Therefore, it’s suggested not to delay 
the beginning of the analysis due to quality of initial source data.  Valuable observations, 
conclusions and recommendations are possible even with poor-quality information. Initial wins will 
boost morale and encourage the maintenance organization to improve data accuracy and improve 
the quality of recommendations and action plans that can be implemented. 
 
Attached is a list of recommended actions, performed on a monthly basis: 
1. Get data from planning department. 
2. Analyze data: 
• Identify top issues per product, frequency and duration. 
• Identify outliers, significant failures/ incidents. 
• Prioritize analysis of products which not reaching availability/ reliability goal. 
3. Gather information about top unavailability contributors: significant failures/ incidents review 
technical reports and maintenance records. 
4. Identity opportunities, gaps, actions required and prepare recommendations for customer, 
dealer, Caterpillar.  
5. If needed, conduct an internal meeting to agree on action plan with different areas (parts 
department, CRC, technical department, Caterpillar).

4.0 Benefits 
 
• Improve availability by optimizing resource utilization in areas with highest opportunity. 
• Dealer, customer, Caterpillar work towards the same goals and align priorities. 
• Improved relationship and trust with the customer. 
• Data based approach allows efficient use of resource on the most urgent issues. 
 
5.0 Resources Required 
 
• Agreement with customer to share maintenance data. 
• Dispatch and/ or maintenance system to document downtime. 
• Process owner can be Site Manager, Site Performance Manager or Reliability Engineer.
