1.0 Introduction  
 
Most Mining customers require an inventory of exchange major components to support their 
production machines, primarily haul trucks and shovels.  The inventory of exchange components 
can be owned by the customer, common for small fleets of equipment, or owned by the dealer as 
part of a major component exchange program, a common practice for large fleets of equipment. 
 
While the purpose of a component exchange inventory is to minimize machine downtime, the 
costs to carry quantities of spare components can be significant. A typical set of components to 
support a fleet of Off Highway Trucks would include the engine, torque converter, transmission, 
differential, front and rear wheel groups, front and rear suspension cylinders, and hoist cylinders. 
For a 793, the seed cost of a set of new components for an exchange program exceeds 
$900,000. 
 
Determining how many exchange components are required to support a fleet of machines can 
depend on several key variables, but an accurate assessment considering all the factors 
impacting component demand can help to minimize and manage inventory costs. 
 
This Best Practice summarizes factors to consider in the evaluation of component exchange 
inventory levels. Due to the complexity of factors to consider and the costs involved, component 
exchange inventory management makes a good 6Sigma project. 
 
2.0 Best Practice Description 
 
Many dealers supporting large mining customers use a “rule of thumb” guide to determine how 
many major components to stock in an exchange program based on the number of machines to 
be supported. The “rule of thumb” guidelines vary from one dealer to the next, but the most 
common practice is to stock one set of major components for every 10 machines. A few dealers 
follow a practice up to one set of components for every 15 - 18 machines.  
 
While a “rule of thumb” guideline maybe easy to 
manage, there is a high cost to stock exchange 
components. Few dealers have actually 
quantified the “ideal” number of components 
“required” to meet expected consumption and 
provide backup for unplanned component 
repairs. 
 
Most dealers think in terms of a “set” of 
components for machine, and not in terms of 
individual components.  For example, a fleet 
may not consume as many fronts wheel groups 
as rear wheel groups due to the life of the 
components, therefore the same quantities of 
each component may not be required. Another

Evaluating requirements and adjusting quantities in inventory based on the requirement and key 
driving factors can save substantial investment costs, better utilize assets, all the while meeting 
the availability needs of the customer. 
 
  
  
 
Managing an exchange component inventory becomes fairly simple once a process is 
established which determines quantities of components and which components are required to 
support a mining fleet of a given size.  Establishing a component exchange management plan is 
best performed as a 6Sigma project due to the multiple variables, cost, and risk involved. 
Important to the process is establishing a process owner, someone responsible for the 
management of component exchange inventory. 
 
Key factors to consider: 
 
• Customer Availability Requirements and Guarantees  
Guarantees with financial penalties involved pose a risk that may justify higher exchange 
inventory investment.

• Machine Population  
It is important to define how many machines of a given model and arrangement are being 
supported by major component exchange components. As populations change, stock 
quantities may need to be adjusted. 
 
• Component Life  
What is the local experience as to average life of each component? Components with 
shorter average component life may require more spare components than components 
with a long component life. 
Customer fluid cleanliness and maintenance practices, along with application severity, 
have significant impact on component life. 
 
• Equipment Monitoring and Maintenance Practices  
A big factor is “planned” rebuilds versus “unplanned” rebuilds. Planned rebuilds can be 
scheduled with some flexibility and are easier to manage.  Unplanned rebuilds are difficult 
to manage. In general, if less than 80% of the component rebuild activity is planned, there 
will be a requirement to increase inventories to protect against surprises.  
 
Customer monitoring practices such as machine inspections, VIMS monitoring, particle 
count and SOS monitoring, and magnetic plug inspections impact the ability to plan before-
failure rebuilds. 
 
• Component Reliability  
Components that have a history of high reliability pose less risk of an unplanned 
breakdown and therefore may require less protective inventory. 
 
• CRC Component Rebuild Turnaround Time  
This is the amount of time it takes a CRC to rebuild a component. For smaller components, 
which may only require 4 to 6 days to rebuild, the risk of machine downtime is lower and 
fewer components would be required for stock. If a spare exchange component is 
consumed, it can be replaced within a few days by the CRC. 
 
Components with a long rebuild time, such as engines, carry increased risk and may 
dictate a higher stock level. 
 
Shop turnaround time is defined as the number of days it takes to complete the rebuild of a 
component. This measurement takes all shop delays into account, such as ordering and 
receiving parts, single versus multiple shifts, machining /salvage delays, etc. 
 
Note : Delays in the CRC should be reviewed for opportunities to improve the turnaround 
time. If engine block salvage is a major rebuild delay, it may be more economical to stock 
a spare block rather than a spare engine. The same may be true for other key parts, such 
as spindles.

When a new fleet of machines is sold, the initial stock of spare components is primarily 
needed to protect machine availability against the risk of an unplanned component 
breakdown.  This “protective” stock can be a minimal stock. 
 
The machines will not enter a planned component rebuild cycle until approximately two 
years after initial machine delivery.  During a rebuild cycle, more exchange component 
stock may be required depending on how many components will be rebuilt, over what time 
frame, and how long it takes to rebuild and turnaround a component in the CRC. 
 
• Cat Reman  
If Caterpillar Remanufactured components are considered as a back up to CRC peak 
component rebuild cycles or unplanned rebuilds, then the time to order and receive a 
Reman component is a factor.  Short lead-time can reduce inventory requirements by 
covering any temporary shortfalls.  Long lead time would mean Reman components could 
be a back up plan for peak CRC workloads, but not for unplanned rebuilds. 
 
• Advance Scheduling  
The ability of a customer and dealer team to communicate and schedule rebuilds in 
advance can be an important critical success factor.  This enables inventory assets to be 
assigned to a scheduled activity, and provides an advance warning if there is a coming 
shortage situation. 
 
• CRC Capacity and Volume   
Based on machine population and scheduled component rebuild activity, what is the normal 
number of any given component that would be in process at the same time?  Is exchange 
component inventory level matched to a monthly in process volume? 
 
• Days at Risk  
How often does the inventory of a particular exchange component fall to zero in stock and 
how many days is the inventory at zero?  This represents “days at risk” when if there is an 
unplanned breakdown, no spare component is available.  Measuring the days at risk and 
establishing an “acceptable” level makes for an important part of the analysis. 
 
Analyzing the above factors, an optimum inventory level can be determined which both 
supports the planned demand for exchange components and protects against the risk of 
unplanned breakdowns. 
 
The following is a very simplified analysis meant to illustrate the basic principles. 
 
A large mining customer orders a fleet of twenty five (25) 785C trucks. These are the first 785s 
in the territory and the dealer has no exchange components in stock. The machines will 
operate 7000 hours per year. The application is moderate. The customer uses the dealer for all 
component rebuilds and expects an exchange component program to support his fleet. 
 
The machines will be delivered over a 12-month period, therefore the planned component 
rebuilds would be expected to occur over a 12-month period.

The most important aspect of managing exchange component inventory is to have a defined 
ownership and a defined process. The owner is someone responsible for inventory 
management and the process is the analysis steps and approval procedure to adjust inventory 
levels. 
 
3.0 Implementation Steps 
 
Due to the complexity of analyzing exchange inventory stocking requirements and the costs 
associated with carrying the inventory, it is highly recommended to use 6Sigma to conduct the 
analysis and establish a process for keeping it current. It is important up front to establish an 
owner for managing the exchange component inventory. 
 
The goal is to support customer planned and unplanned exchange component requirements at a 
minimum carrying cost and acceptable inventory turnover.

6Sigma steps include: 
 
Define 
Create a team, set goals and objectives of the team, look at any existing inventory management 
process, and determine customer requirements. 
 
Measure 
Collect data on mining machine populations, annual volume of planned and unplanned rebuilds, 
CRC component rebuild turnaround time, inventory levels and turnover, rebuild schedules, CRC 
capacity, and the data supporting the factors addressed in the above “Description” section. 
Analyze the risk for both the customers and the dealership. 
 
Analyze 
Develop solutions that match planned and unplanned component demand to inventory and CRC 
capacity using data collected in “Measure”. Identify all factors impacting demand and supply. 
 
Implement 
Establish the process, determine optimum stocking inventory, and adjust inventory over time to 
the target levels.  
 
Control 
Ensure that new process meets customer needs. Monitor and adjust inventories as machine 
population and other factors change. 
 
4.0 Benefits 
 
• Protect Mining customer machine availability and p roduction. 
• Create preference and loyalty for dealer CRC suppo rt. 
• Manage CRC workflow. 
• Minimize component exchange inventory investment a nd carrying costs. 
• Support flat rate repair options. 
• Grow rebuild business and revenue. 
• Utilize dealer CRC and investment assets efficient ly. 
• Minimizes cost impact of product design changes on  inventory components 
• Improves inventory turnover ratio. 
 
5.0 Resources Required 
 
Exchange Component Inventory Management makes an excellent 6Sigma project that is 
relatively quick to complete and has immediate results.  A key step to creating the process is to 
determine ownership, someone who is responsible for managing dealer component inventory 
practices.
