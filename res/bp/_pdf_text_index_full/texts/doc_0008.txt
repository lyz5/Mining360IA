1.0 Introduction  
 
Hydraulic mining shovels, electric rope shovels, draglines and rotary drills work deep inside the 
pit, which is generally far away from the main workshop. Tramming these machines would prove 
to be very time consuming and costly. In some cases, additional equipment and labor must be 
brought in – For example, a water truck would be required to water down the undercarriage to 
prevent overheating and subsequent failure of track rollers. Therefore, service work on these 
machines must be performed within the pit in a designated flat area (shovel pad/drill pad) 
 
This Best Practice describes the use of an in-pit workshop that can be placed at the shovel pad or 
drill pad. Additional considerations are also included to the health & safety of technicians. As the 
active mining faces progress, this workshop can be moved to a more suitable position. 
 
 
2.0 Best Practice Description 
 
Instead of working under the weather or sheltering in an empty container, an in-pit workshop can 
be built and placed at the shovel pad to execute service work faster and more efficiently. The in-
pit workshop can either be self-built locally, bought as a standard unit, or built by a supplier to 
specifications. 
 
The pictures in this best practice show one unit built by a local supplier in Australia. The pictures 
are reproduced with permission from the supplier and Caterpillar does not endorse this specific 
supplier. It is simply shown as an example of the possibilities. 
 
Figure 1 shows a standard ready-made unit with side opening doors – Converted from a standard 
container, this option allows for easy transportation. The doors can be locked in the open 
position.  When opened, they act as side walls on both sides and have fold-down work benches.  
A roof is placed at the top to shed the container from a harsh sun for better cooling.  Extendable 
awnings can be wound out to extend the shaded area.  A lockable store room is built in for 
storage of tools and parts. 
 
Figure 2 shows a separate unit which is a combined wash room and lunch room.  Separate units 
can be introduced for higher occupancy rates. Access ramps provide safe entry and exit to both 
areas.  They can be built as foldable units or removal and storage in the lunch room for 
transportation.  The air conditioning unit is flushed with the external side wall to avoid damage 
during transportation.

3.0 Implementation Steps 
 
To implement such a project, a champion needs to be elected to research and manage the 
process of introducing such a setup. 
 
i. Research on suitable suppliers and products available. 
ii. Obtain budgetary pricing with contingency costs for justification. 
iii. Develop justification costing model. 
iv. Research on requirements by technicians. 
v. Determine power requirements for suitable gen set. 
vi. Research on site requirements and legislations. 
vii. Review suitability and availability of equipment for transporting modules. 
viii. Select suitable supplier. 
ix. Develop and finalize on requirements of the modules with supplier. 
x. Obtain authorization to purchase. 
xi. Develop instructions on usage of the workshop. 
xii. Develop instructions for starting and shutting down of gen set. 
xiii. When delivered, commission and conduct training on usage of the whole setup. 
xiv. Move to desired location. 
 
 
4.0 Benefits 
 
There are a few different benefits in using such a setup. 
i. Savings in technician labor due to availability of common parts being stored in the mobile 
workshop. 
ii. Savings in technician labor with less frequent traveling to and from the main workshop 
when work involves component repairs. 
iii. Improved work quality that can impact reliability and improve MTBS (Mean Time Between 
Shutdowns). 
iv. Lower downtime so that machine can get back to work earlier for higher production. 
 
As an example, assume: 
A shovel moving 3,000 tonnes of material per hour in a gold mine with a 1:4 strip ratio. 
Ore has 10gm gold per tonne and gold recovery is 85%. 
Utilization is 70%. 
400 repair jobs per year, 40% of which can make use of the in-pit workshop to an advantage. 
Savings of 15 minutes for each of these jobs. 
 
Additional hours available = 400 x 40% x (15/60) x 70% = 28 hours per year 
Additional ore moved   = (3000 ÷ 5) x 28 = 16,800 tonnes per year 
Additional gold production = 16,800 x (10 ÷ 31) x 85% = 4,606 oz gold per year 
Additional benefit @ $1200 per ounce = 4,606 x 1200 = $5.5M per year

5.0 Resources Required 
 
The project of introducing this setup on site requires a Champion.  The Champion is required to 
source and cost the project.  The benefits can then be presented to management for approval.  
When approved, the Champion will oversee the development of detailed specifications, build-up, 
purchase, document development and commissioning of the setup.  In most instances, the site 
should already have a shovel or drill pad in the vicinity of the load area.  If this is not the case, the 
Champion will have to work with Production and Mine Engineers on location of such an area.
