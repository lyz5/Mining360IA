1.0 Introduction  
 
The useable life of a mining machine is often governed by the integrity of the frame and/or other 
major structures. By introducing a program to effectively manage these structures, there is a 
potential to significantly extend the life of the equipment, which in turn allows sites to defer capital 
expenditure and ultimately reduce the Total Cost of Ownership (TCO) of their equipment. 
 
This Best Practice provides a high-level overview of a complete management of structures 
program that focuses on the process to support the identification, tracking and repair of structural 
defects within structures. This acute focus on locations and sources of excessive stresses can 
support all parties within the supply chain. 
 
2.0 Best Practice Description 
 
The development of an effective Management of Structures program requires a clear 
understanding of the contributors and responsible parties that will ultimately influence the 
serviceable life of a major structure: 
 
1.  Machine Design  
 
The manufacturer is responsible to design a structure will include economical, and 
manufacturability criteria.  The overarching expectation for structure design is to meet or 
exceed the design life based off the loads and stresses that the machine will be subjected to 
under typical operations within a mining operational environment. 
 
2. Machine Quality 
 
The manufacturer is required to assemble and weld the structures to meet quality standards 
that are defined by industry and/or within the business where appropriate. 
 
3. Operational Envelope 
 
The manufacturer will provide operational manuals that specify the operating conditions for 
which the machine has been designed.  This is often supported though onboard sensors and 
alarms to identify where a threshold has been exceeded along with ongoing operator training 
and coaching.   
  
The customer should endeavor to operate the machine as per the provided guidance; 
however, they may still experience events that exceed the designed stress limitations of the 
structures.  Events such as inadvertent contact with the stops when curling a bucket, 
contacting the face or other objects while still slewing, haul road condition, overloading, 
uncontrolled face movement etc.  All these examples may have a negative influence the 
structure durability.  
 
Operate for reliability (O4R) programs are highly recommended as part of a structural 
management program. Machine reliability is significantly impacted by how the machine is 
operated, therefore, identification and monitoring of the alarms that are generated by incorrect

4. Condition Monitoring 
 
A good condition monitoring and inspection program is recommended to ensure that any 
cracks that are developing within frames are identified as early as possible. This allows for 
repairs to be managed before the magnitude of the task becomes significant.   
 
The Operation and Maintenance Manual (O&MM) for Caterpillar mining equipment provides 
recommendation for the frequency for which the frame should be inspected.  This inspection 
guidance also identifies specific areas should be inspected on a regular basis due to known 
stresses and loading in the equipment.    
 
5. Repair Management 
 
Once a defect (crack or potentially mechanically induced damage) has been identified within 
the structure it is critical to ensure that a quality repair is performed as soon is reasonably 
practicable.   
 
Whoever conducts this repair should: 
• Be appropriately trained with demonstrated competency 
• Have the correct tools and setup for the task 
• Follow repair instructions for specific repairs as provided by the 
manufacturer 
• Have an adequate Quality Assurance (QA) process / system in place to 
verify repair quality

6. Current Process Gaps 
 
The current inspection sheets that are used by many customers will provide a snapshot of the 
frame condition and feed into their repair processes.  When challenged however, many 
customers will struggle to identify details that could benefit their current programs. 
 
When evaluating the current situation for your site are you able to answer the following 
questions: 
• Where exactly are the cracks identified on your equipment? 
• Is cracking limited to a few select machines or all similar equipment in the fleet? 
• How frequently are repairs being performed? 
• What is the status and rate of change of the defect? E.g. new, growing, doubled 
in a week. 
• Which areas were inspected during last PM? 
• Do inspectors know what areas to inspect? 
• What criteria defines when a repair is required? 
• What is the required repair procedure? 
• Are there factory updates available to address problematic areas?   
• Is it more cost effective to repair reactively?  Invest in updates?  O r should site 
consider replacing that piece of equipment? 
• Were the previous repairs as per OEM recommendations? 
 
 
Examples of summary report detail following frame inspections 
 
3.0 Implementation Steps 
 
1.  Detailed Inspection Document with Locations 
 
The frames should be examined in detail and where possible each area of the machine be 
assigned a unique zone identification and where possible each weld location on the frame be 
individually identified.  This naming structure allows for further customization and addition of 
inspection locations as required throughout the life of the equipment.

Location Identification Structure:  CCCC-ZZPP-IP 
 
CCCC – 4 Digit Service Management Control System (SMCS) Code – Note that for 
majority of frames this will be: 7050  
 
 ZZ – Zone - High level breakdown of frames into zones, such that every part of the 
structure fits into a zone.

identify more specifically where in the zone that inspection is reviewing  
 
 IP – Inspection point – This is a specific area within that frame that is being reviewed  
This can be a weld, casting area, mounting block or any unique feature that may exhibit 
cracking throughout the life of the equipment.  
 
There are inherent complexities in a numbering system that will cover all types of 
equipment however the intent is to create a database for each model that then allows 
tracking of all zones / locations across the machine.

2. Development of inspection schedule for each machine  
 
• Basic visual of high priority areas should be inspected every 500 hrs (or per 
guidance provided within the machine O&MM).   
• Detailed frame inspections should be performed every 6 months utilizing appropriate 
non destructive testing procedures such as magnetic particle, ultrasonic, dye 
penetrant or visual – There will be areas on a frame that will remain inaccessible 
during this inspection.  
• Major PCR intervals – With components removed this provides the greatest 
opportunity to fully evaluate the health of the structures and repair during these 
events.   
 
3. Database Tracking 
 
Sites will need to develop a database for which the cracks can be documented.  As 
inspections are performed the detail should be captured in the database for further analysis.  
 
This database should maintain a reference between defects identified and the Location 
Identification Structure

4. Analysis of Data 
 
As required the information captured within the structured database should be reviewed.  The 
purpose of these reviews can vary significantly from qualification that existing repairs are 
effective, identification of problematic areas, upgrade evaluation etc. 
 
At a high level however having robust data will allow sites to verify if frames and major 
structures are fatiguing and where possible develop strategies to support extended machine 
life.  
 
Where cracking to the same area across multiple units is detected, analysis should be 
undertaken to determine root cause of the cracking (e.g. batch defect, weld defect, haul road 
design, operating procedures, change in environmental conditions, etc.). 
 
4.0 Benefits 
 
The financial benefits of a structural management program will vary dramatically between sites 
and customers based on high level fleet management strategies.  There are four main areas for 
which benefits of this program will be observed by customers. 
 
1. Safety  
 
Unexpected failure of structures in large mobile plant can result in serious injuries to 
the operators or others in the vicinity of the equipment.  Ensuring that the equipment 
is fit for purpose should always be the number 1 priority. 
 
2. Capital Avoidance  
 
Extending machine life can provide sites with significant capital avoidance benefits.   
Deferring a $6 million machine purchase with extremely low interest rates (3%) 
will avoid $180,000 in interest rates per annum.  Taxes and capital incentives may 
influence the total potential value of this deferral.   
 
 
3. Improved Availability 
 
Crack repairs are often undertaken in a reactive manner, where the crack, once 
identified, is hastily “patched” up and the machine is placed back into service without 
understanding and addressing the root cause behind the crack. This method is 
perceived to be ‘easier’ and can include repairs from the exposed /outside of the 
frame without complete removal / repair that may be required from the inside of the 
structure. In these events sites may see numerous repeated repairs where a single 
extended shutdown will result in a better repair that will produce increased 
availability. 
 
Once identified, the crack should be assessed and controlled as required. This may 
require immediate repair, or planned into the next repair window (e.g. service) with
