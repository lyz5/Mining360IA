1.0 Introduction  
 
Due to limited ground clearances, service pits are now a common practice with underground 
mining workshops.  Upon completion of service, the pits can present a potential falling hazard.  
To mitigate this risk, sites commonly use barriers or plates.  Either approach involves heavy lifting 
or use of forklifts. 
  
This Best Practice details a pneumatically operated barrier that moves into place and retracts for 
service with a simple switch.  This system provides a safety barrier to the pit without heavy lifting 
and also saves time required with construction. 
 
 
2.0 Best Practice Description 
 
The barrier is divided into two sections.  The proportion of the two sections can be adjusted per 
site requirements.  However, the shorter section needs to be long enough to have two hinged 
points to properly leverage it in and out of the way.  One section must have the intersecting 
portion made narrower to fit within the opposite section when collapsed into the pit; to avoid an 
interference of the two sections.  Image 1 shows the barrier in the erected position. 
 
 
Image 1: Pit barrier in the erected position 
 
Both sections are operated by dedicated pneumatic actuators.  They can also be plumbed to 
operate independently if required.  When the barrier is folded down, the sections sit below the 
floor level to limit obstruction.  This prevents the barrier itself from being damaged by parts of the 
machines moving over the pit.  Image 2 shows the barrier already folded down.

Safety Concerns 
• Various pinch points exist with this solution.  Booth Engineering has agreed to provide 
documentation on this upon request 
• Can the barrier collapse unexpectedly? 
o The pneumatic cylinders hold everything in raised position and lowered position. It 
doesn't lock in place, the air pressure is sufficient to hold it very firmly in place. 
o The pneumatic cylinders are fitted with pilot check valves. If the air compressor is 
turned off overnight and pressure runs down, the pilot check valves ensure that the 
air is held in the bottom end of the cylinders, thereby keeping the rails raised. The 
pilot check valves require a positive pressure to allow them to release air from the 
cylinders. The only way to lower them is to operate the main control valve when air 
pressure has been built up. 
 
 
4.0 Benefits 
 
1. Safety against personnel falling into service pits. 
2. Eliminate heavy manual handling by other methods of pit barriers. 
3. Reduce labor time requirement to set up and take down manual barriers. 
 
5.0 Resources Required 
 
Cost of installation is varied, depending on the structure of the pit lining for attachment of 
mounting points.  Cost also depends on any required modifications such as the staircase top for 
the barrier to fit below and surface and also avoid tripping of personnel when using the staircase.
