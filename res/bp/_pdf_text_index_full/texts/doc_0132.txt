1.0 Introduction  
 
Haul road design and maintenance is an important part of a mine site’s overall performance. Not only 
does it impact cycle times and productivity, but also has a bearing on the life of truck components 
such as the transmission, duo-cone seals and most costly, the machine frame. It is often difficult to 
explain the long-term effects of haul road conditions with pictures, FELA numbers or R.A.C. data 
alone. This Best Practice outlines a method to produce videos overlaid with gauges and graphs 
showing live data as experienced directly by a truck travelling on the haul road or dump area.   
It should be used in conjunction with other haul road improvement analysis tools by mine site 
reliability engineers, dealer MARC managers and application engineers to provide mine site 
managers with a visual “as is” picture of what a machine is experiencing on a day to day basis on 
their own haul roads.   
Videos produced with this Best Practice can also be produced and included in the Application section 
of a mine site assessment when reviewing haul roads with mine management.  Videos can then 
easily be shared with the enterprise to sensitize people to specific haul road issues such as slope 
consistency, speed on grade, overloading, drainage or even truck-shovel placement.

MINING DEALER BEST PRACTICE  
 
The key Elements of an effective video involve: 
• Defining the Purpose of the Video for the selected minesite 
• Acquiring the right hardware for onsite filming and data collection 
• Planning the video filming and data acquisition with the minesite 
• Setting up on the machine and collecting the data 
• Using overlay and editing software to produce a quality video 
• Distributing the message  
 
 
3.0 Implementation Steps 
 
3.1 Defining the Purpose of the Video for the selected minesite 
 
This is the most important step in the process of delivering an effective message to the audience.  
Regardless of the quality of the data and footage achieved, the purpose of the video must be defined 
and understood.  Agreeing on a set purpose the mine management will also help securing access to 
machines and other resources during filming.  Haulroad maintenance may not be the only priority 
issue and this tool can help explain many other situations 
The following are some questions to begin with: 
1. Is there a clear burning platform? 
• We need to communicate to operators to slow down during the fall in order to reduce 
tire cuts. 
• There is a lot traffic on the haul road, other vehicles are not yielding to production 
trucks, causing longer cycle times. 
• We are reporting many RAC events and we believe the haul road in the ‘C’ pit is 
poorly maintained. 
2. Are there known situations that have been reported? 
• Overspills, large boulders left in the road 
• Water, ineffective drainage, puddles 
• Operator discomfort due to dips and harsh turns 
• Truck Bunching, long wait times at the loading areas 
• Unequal loads, bailing of material 
• Contact with the berm 
• Tire cuts, tire wear or delamination 
• Incidents, collisions, or close calls in some areas of the haul road 
3. Are there two conditions to compare? 
• Good haul road versus poorly maintained haul road 
• Good use of the retarder versus improper use of the retarder 
• Speed on grade of different operators, different truck models 
Answering these questions will allow better planning when selecting the correct framing of 
the videos and the limited number of data channels to sample.  Don’t assume that all you 
will need is a shot from the front of the machine.  A camera tucked under the truck can 
capture the stress a strut goes under running on a poorly maintained road. Another camera 
facing an operator’s seat can capture how much bouncing around the operator goes 
through during a cycle.

3.2 Acquiring the right hardware for onsite filming and data acquisition 
3.2.1 Video Cameras 
 
Figure 1 GoPro Hero 2 
 
The most popular heavy duty camera platform at the time of writing this Best Practice is 
the GoPro series of cameras.  All our videos to date have been filmed with several GoPro 
cameras. These are designed for extreme sports and the motorsports mounts are a good 
fit for mining and truck mounting. If GoPro cameras are not available, look for cameras 
with the following features: 
 
• C ompatibility with versatile mounting systems 
• Availability of accessories (online, local store) 
• Battery life 
• Picture Quality

3.2.2 Mounting hardware 
 
Figure 2: Motorsports Accessory and Handlebar Accessory 
Within the GoPro series of mounts is the motorsports accessory which uses a strong 
vacuum cup and works well when mounting on flat parts of the machine such as windows 
and smooth painted surfaces. The length of the arm included with the accessory allows 
the user to position the camera easily.   You can also use the handlebar accessory shown 
on the left of Figure 2.  This mount secures to piping such as Catwalks.  Make sure to 
secure the mount to the lower parts of the catwalk to avoid excessive vibration.  You will 
also need to use extension arms in order to pivot the camera correctly.  The GoPro sliding 
mount can also be adapted to fit to common ¼” threaded camera mounts as shown in 
Figure 3 where a ¼” nut is stuck onto the back of a standard Gopro camera receptacle 
This mount is useful when trying to reach out to an area clear of the mounting point, such 
as over the top of the canopy.

The GoPro cameras have a tendency to turn on when they bounce around.  This

3.2.4 Data acquisition hardware 
 
The hardware below will allow you to gather the data in raw form to be used with the overlay 
software.  We have typically used Caterpillar Electronic Technician with a Comm adapter to 
link to the machine’s ECM’s.  The software used to overlay the data is also capable of plotting 
the position of the machine as well as graphing acceleration forces captured by another 
device.  There are inexpensive phone applications that are easy to use that can gather this 
data if needed.  We have successfully used GPS Tracks App for iPhone by DMorneault to 
gather GPS position and combine in RaceRender 2 to map out the course where the machine 
is located.

3.3 Planning each task with the minesite 
Planning is also a very important part of this process, a minesite is a large complex operation 
and a careful plan will mitigate the effect of the study on production.   A single truck out of 
normal production represents a large investment therefor setup time should be kept at a 
minimum. Below is an example of a typical schedule. 
  
8:00 or Shift Change Meet with foreman and operators that will be affected 
8:30 Travel to machine meet point 
9:45 Install camera gear, setup datalogger 
10:00 Begin first take 
11:00 End first take , Check data 
11:15 Begin Second take 
12:00 End Second take 
    
When meeting with the foreman, take note of any exceptions that are made to regular 
operating practices such as maximum speed, maximum gear, target payload.  The mine may 
choose to exceptionally raise or lower machine speeds or request the operator perform his 
task differently than usual.  They may also remove all traffic from the selected route to 
measure the effect of a clear road or remove unwanted stoppages. 
You will need about fifteen minutes to mount the camera gear on the machine as well as to 
setup the laptop, choose the datalog channels and setup the sampling rate and duration. If 
you are onsite for a few days for a site assessment, try selecting a day with no rain/snow as 
mud will cover the camera and snow may obstruct the view.  Try to take into account the sun’s 
position at that time to avoid glare that could make the picture unusable.  It is important to 
know what ECM data channels you will be sampling ahead of time as it can take much time to 
go through the list.  Machines today have over one hundred channels to choose from and you 
will need to select the right channels that will provide valuable information.  For a list of 
available ECM channels, you can look them up for your selected machine in CMS at 
https://catcms.cat.com  under Manual Analysis and Datalog/SS, or contact the DSN or 
technical communicator for a list.  You can also check with the mine if a similar machine is 
available in the shop for you to setup on prior to the study day.

3.4 Setting up on the machine and collecting the data 
 
The day before filming, make sure that you charge all the batteries for the cameras and store 
them where they are unlikely of being inadvertently turned on. Check that Caterpillar Electronic 
Technician loads up well and that your license is active. Fully charge your laptop and your phone. 
Put the cameras, mounts, comm adaptor and laptop a in a bag with shoulder straps. 
Taking the video and datalog 
This is the easiest part, but first use every precaution when working around a machine: 
• Always use three points of contact 
• Carry all your gear in a shoulder bag, you will not have any free hands as you climb on 
the machine 
• Make sure the operator acknowledges your presence and is aware of what you are 
doing, request a radio if possible 
• Follow the machine lock-out processes for the areas of the machine you are 
addressing 
 
4. Mount the camera according to the type of mounting hardware selected, do not start the 
camera.  Consider weather mud will reach the camera. If the area surrounding the 
mounting location looks clean, chances are the camera will be clean for a while  
5. Preview the shot (optional).  If you have connected a phone with the preview app, position 
the camera for the optimal framing.  Figure 4 shows a typical setup 
6. Remove the camera from the housing.  In order to start the datalogger and the camera at 
the same time, you should take the camera in the cab with you.  Make sure you don’t 
move the housing so as not to change the framing 
7. Setup Electronic Technician. Figure 5 shows a screenshot of a typical setup,  
• Set sample rate to 240 per minute 
• Set Duration to 60 minutes or more, this must be long enough to allow the time for a 
full cycle plus the time to get to the start of the cycle 
• Set the Pre-Trigger to 0, we will be starting the datalog in sync with the camera; a 
pre-trigger will insert a delay and must be removed. 
• Note the location of the datalogger file and name for later  
8. Select the data channels.  Figure 6 shows channels for overlaying haul road information. 
You can save the list of channels in ET and it will be available when you select the same 
ECM again on that machine. 
9. Setup your GPS tracker (optional).  If you’re including a map and the position of the 
machine in the overlay design, setup the GPS logger according to the instructions in the 
user manual.  A sample rate of 1 second should be enough, make sure the accuracy is 
acceptable, within ±10meters is enough for a haul road application. Figure 7 shows the 
start screen of the app.

10. Critical: Start all three processes at the same time, press start on the phone, start on the 
ET screen, and press down on the top camera button all at once.  You can sync up the 
timing of the video and the data (GPS and ET) later within RaceRender, however starting 
all three at once will save much time.  Have the machine operator help you and 
countdown.  If this is not possible for you, take careful note of the time difference between 
the start of the video and the start of the datalog.   
11. Place your phone and laptop in a secure location, with the phone facing the window and 
preferably clipped to the visor or cab liner. Be careful not to close the laptop lid or pull the 
USB cable out. 
12. Place the camera back into the housing  
13. Start moving the machine.  At this point you can let the operator go alone, or accompany 
him/her to make sure the devices don’t fall or stop.  Keep track of time, as the datalogger 
will stop recording once its time has run out. 
14. At the end of the cycle, you can stop the datalogger, GPS recording and camera.  You do 
not have to stop every device at the same time. 
15. Check that the files have saved successfully.  Check that the files open properly, you may 
need to do another take, 
16. Travel to your drop point and collect all your gear

3.6 Distributing the message within the proper medium 
 
When carefully planned, the filming and data acquisition should not take longer than half a 
day, leaving enough time to create at least a draft of the video within a full day for presenting 
at an approval meeting the next day.  The mine management may want to make some 
changes to the displayed information to better suit their audience.  Take this feedback into 
consideration when preparing the final version of the video. 
Caterpillar uses 
https://videoatwork.cat.com, a media sharing website to securely share media 
such as these videos with internal Caterpillar and dealer personnel. The file will likely be larger 
than 500MB and can be sent with Caterpillar’s Secure Internet File Transfer tool at 
https://SIFT.cat.com for internal Caterpillar and dealer transfers.  For sharing the video with 
customers, leave a copy of the video with the customer in order for them to share within the 
organization.  Respect the customer’s data privacy protocols. 
 
4.0 Resources Required 
• Time: 
o Half a day prior the shooting to plan, meet with the mine site, charge batteries and 
gather equipment 
o Depending on the length of time for a cycle, plan on at least two takes, plus 20 minutes 
to mount the hardware, log into Caterpillar Electronic Technician and setup the 
datalogger.  Usually no more than 3 hours total per segment. 
o Setting up of the overlay design can take up to 8 hours, but a draft can be provided 
within 2 hours
