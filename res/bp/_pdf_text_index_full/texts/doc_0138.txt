1.0 Introduction  
Safety is an extremely important topic to companies that operate in and around the mining industry.  There are a 
variety of safety procedures and policies in use around the world at mining companies and mining dealers. The 
procedures documented here were taken from a mining customer site that had reported success in reducing the 
number of reportable incidents. It is advised to the reader that there are extensive variations to the procedures 
documented here in use around the world and that this summary is offered as one particular example that has 
worked for a mining company. As additional examples of procedures are documented from other mining companies, 
it is anticipated they will be added here.  
 
Reviewing current fatality and incident data clearly establishes the need for a sound safety policy at a mine site. 
Readily available data comes from the U.S. government’s Mine Safety and Health Administration (MSHA) website.  
MSHA collects and publishes incident related data from mine sites throughout the U.S. The data revealed here is 
from 2005.

Reviewing the fatality data from MSHA for 2005 and non-fatal incidents from 2003, it is clear that additional work is 
needed to improve safety at mine sites. One method of achieving the objective of zero incidents is the development 
of well established, followed, and managed safety procedures. This Best Practice  will focus on concepts to achieve 
effective safety procedures.
4 
 
This Best Practice deals with mine site safety practices and processes that can be used to reduce and minimize 
safety incidents. Safety incident reporting is sometimes considered taboo and is often the result of a miss 
understanding of mine management’s intent and philosophy. A properly developed safety culture at a mine site would 
include full support, full reporting, and a vigorous attitude towards implementing and using good safety practices and 
processes.  
 
This Best Practice will outline high level practices and processes seen in use at a mine sites around the world. It will 
not provide target metrics or site-specific implementation recommendations as that is entirely up to the reader to 
consider and implement as needed This Best Practice is meant to make the reader aware of what practices and 
processes should be in place at a mine site. Ultimately, the key benefit of implementing processes outlined in this 
Best Practice is for enhancing operator and technician safety.

2.0 Best Practice Description 
 
2.1 Philosophy 
 
2.1.1 Safety Vision 
A safety vision can be a simple phrase or statement used to summarize an organization’s entire underlying safety 
philosophy. It serves as a daily visual and verbal reminder to all employees that safety is first in everything they 
do. It helps employees understand that everything done will use will focus on achieving safety. A successful 
safety program has engagement and true participation from all levels of the mine site. A particular mine’s actual 
safety vision may differ slightly, but the underlying intent is the same. The vision should be written and well 
communicated throughout the mine site. 
 
2.1.2 Without Harm to People, Property, Environment or Reputation 
In support of a safety vision, it must understand that actions to achieve this philosophy must also protect people, 
property, environment, and reputation.  
 
2.2 Individual Pre-Site Access: 
 
2.2.1 Drug and Alcohol Testing 
Drug and alcohol testing is used as part of the employee selection process by assi sting in the determination if the 
potential employee is fit for duty. Drug and alcohol testing is also used post-incident is support of an investigation. 
An off site, independent lab is used to ensure confidentiality and unbiased results.  
 
2.2.2 Medical Fitness Assessment 
A medical physical is given as part of pre-employment analysis to determine if the potential candidate is fit for 
duty and to provide benchmark data for the employee. 
 
2.3 Personal Commitment 
Personal ownership of an employee’s responsibility for safety is important. The mine has reported the use of a 
large poster that outlines the basic safety requirements and is posted in the shop or operations area for all to see. 
The following statements are included on the poster that all employees voluntarily sign.  
• Working safely is a condition of employment when working at the mine 
• I believe all accidents are preventable 
• I am responsible for my own individual safety performance 
• I am responsible for my co-workers safety performance 
• I am responsible for the environment, property and reputation of our workplace 
• I am responsible to stop unsafe acts noticed 
 
2.4 Orientations and Training 
 
2.4.1 Regulatory Required General Safety Training 
Many federal regulations and local governments require the completion of government sponsored mining safety 
training. In the United States, that includes Mine Safety and Health Administration (MSHA) training, in Alberta, 
Canada, this includes Construction Safety Training System (CSTS), and in South Australia, that includes M ining 
and Quarrying Occupational Health and Safety Committee (MAQOHSC) training. This training is conducted prior 
to arriving on site. All employees would have this training completed on the periodic basis required by the local 
regulation. Other examples of regulatory required training is occupational health and safety (OH&S).

Due to extremely unique regional hazards and regional safety requirements, a mine site will conduct a regional 
orientation for all employees. This is a general orientation that focuses on the safety philosophy and general 
requirements that are unique to that area. Regional orientation would include awareness of culture and climate, 
fauna, plants and vegetation, and endangered species as examples. 
 
2.4.3 Site Specific Orientation 
Prior to admittance of any employee or visitor to a mine site, a mine will provide a site- specific safety orientation 
course. The training outlines the expectations of all employees and visitors. The site- specific training will include 
emergency evacuation procedures, emergency contact numbers, emergency alarm types, driving requirements, 
personal protective equipment requirements, mine property and pit limits, signage in use, hazardous areas of the 
mine, any regional or governmental training requirements, and local site conditions, as examples.  
 
2.4.4 Area Specific Orientation 
Once on site, area specific training is also provided for employees and visitors to the very specific requirements 
for a particular part of the mine. This may include reporting or permission requirements to conduct work, confined 
space and welding requirements, or working at heights requirements as examples.  
 
2.4.4.1 Health & Safety, Security, Environmental & Industrial Hygiene 
Each department trains their team members in the rules, procedures, standards and risks related 
specifically to their department. Also included is a review of previous incidents, a department tour, and 
appropriate personal protective equipment etc. 
 
2.4.4.2 Equipment Operation 
In order to operate equipment or vehicles, team members must be trained and certified on each specific 
vehicle or equipment they need to operate. Training can consist of Computer Based Learning (CBL) 
specific to the make and model of the equipment, and then checked via evaluation by a specified trainer. 
This training is required prior to operating all light vehicles, forklifts, skid steers, man lifts, overhead 
cranes, heavy equipment, etc. 
 
2.4.5 Task Specific Training 
This is very similar in scope to Area Specific Training, however it is focused on the very specific tasks used on the 
job. It is expected that team members be trained to complete all tasks – whether it be how to use the wash bay, 
how to change oil, how to apply degreaser, or how to remove an engine. This expectation is meet by providing 
experienced workers to train new employees, and using specific procedures established on site involving 
equipment operation and maintenance. 
 
2.4.6 Hazard Assessment and Recognition Training 
Employees are given a course that teaches workers to identify hazards of each job they are doing, and how to 
control them. There is a tool (Job Safety Analysis) that is used to document this assessment. These assessments 
are done for all work where there are hazards or when a Field Level Risk Assessment (FLRA) would not be 
effective. 
 
2.4.6.1 Field Level Risk Assessments (FLRA) 
Workers are trained to stop and evaluate each task, or each part of a task, to identify safety hazards. This 
assessment is not documented. As an example, an FLRA would be used when a field mechanic was 
called to diagnose a hydraulic leak on a dozer. A field level risk assessment includes stopping prior to 
initiating work, looking around and identifying hazards, assess the risks, control the risks, and then 
resume work.

2.4.6.2 Job Safety Analysis (JSA) 
A formal, documented process used to identify the steps of a job, the potential hazards, and how they will 
be controlled. This must be done for all work where there is a medium or above risk, and all workers 
involved in the job must be involved in the JSA and sign it off. For example, a JSA would be required 
when a worker(s) are assigned to remove and install a front strut on an off-highway truck.

2.4.6.3 Permit to Work 
For high risk tasks (confined space entry, two crane lifts, critical lifts, hot work on tanks, etc) an actual 
permit is required. This is an extensive document that identifies all possible hazards and how they will be 
documented. This is developed with the workers involved, the supervisors, a safety person, a member of 
the emergency response team, and the department manager. The permit is issued by date and time, and 
cannot run past the end of the shift. All workers participating in the task must review and sign it, an d when 
the work is complete all people involved in the development must close and sign the permit.  
 
2.4.6.4 Planned Inspections/Behavior Based Safety Observations 
Hazard recognition training as listed above is required to participate in planned inspections  of the work 
area. This mine site adopted the BEST Behavior Based Safety concept. BEST stands for Building 
Everyone Safe Tomorrows. All employees without exception are required to be trained in the BEST 
principles. Additional information on the BEST Behavior Based Safety concept can be found at 
http://www.y12.doe.gov/news/report/toc.php?vn=1_2

Stored energy must be identified and controlled prior to working on a job. This can include electrical, 
hydraulic, gravitational, pressure, etc. This training and standard ensures workers are able to identify a

2.4.7 Due Diligence Training 
This course teaches the definition of Due Diligence and also stresses the importance of diligent compliance to 
rules and procedures.  The course focuses on ACTION, that is taking a proactive approach to having due 
diligence in everything you do. 
 
2.4.8 Hazardous Material Training 
Many governments require hazardous material training and would be provided to all employees that work with or 
have access to hazardous materials. 
 
2.4.9 New Worker Education 
This is a short course that identifies some of the safety risks that new workers face, and stresses the importance 
of asking questions. 
 
2.4.10 Back Injury Prevention 
This is a short course focusing on maintaining a health back, awareness, and proper lifting techniques.  
 
2.4.11 Waste Management Guidelines 
This training this program focuses on the waste management requirements on site, including waste segregation, 
storage and disposal. 
 
2.4.12 ISO 14001 Awareness 
This program provides a general overview of the ISO 14001 if the mine site has received, and how individuals are 
accountable for the environmental performance. 
 
2.4.13 Team Leader Training 
 
2.4.14.1 Mine Safety Supervisor Education Program 
Training program for supervisors. 
 
2.4.14.2 Risk Assessment Matrix Training 
This training provides an in-depth look at how to use a risk assessment matrix and how incidents are 
rated for actual and potential consequences. 
 
2.4.14.3 Incident Investigation Training 
This provides basic investigation tools, focusing on determining immediate and basic causes, and 
determining corrective actions that are “Action Based.” 
 
2.4.14.4 Incident Reporting System Training 
All incidents are reported in an electronic incident reporting system. The initial facts must be identified, 
reported and communicated electronically throughout the site within the shift the incident occurred using a 
“Flash” report. The investigation must be complete and all action plans completed and documented within 
30 days. Only the team leader who initiates the incident report can update, so they own it from start to 
finish. 
 
2.5 Communications 
 
2.5.1 Asses Meeting Each Shift 
At the start of each shift, all employees must attend a safety meeting to determine if their previous shift was safe, 
and if there are any safety concerns to be raised. This occurs 100% of the time, day, night and weekends – no 
exceptions.

2.5.2 Weekly and/or Monthly Safety Meetings 
Regular meetings are held for each crew to provide safety education and feedback. Note this is not the time to 
bring up safety issues – these are to be dealt with immediately. 
 
2.5.3 Daily Statistical Reports 
Daily site wide safety report for all incident classifications is provided and posted in common areas for all 
employees to see. Attached is also a daily learning session/communication related to safety to share with the 
employees. 
 
2.5.4 Incident Learning Reports 
The generic information from all incidents throughout the site is distributed site wide, to communicate incidents 
and immediate corrective actions taken. The complete details of all significant incidents is circulated in an Incident 
Learning Package to be reviewed with all teams. 
 
2.6 Injury Management 
 
2.6.1 Focus on First Aid/Near Miss Level 
A first aid injury is significant and treated as serious – a worker has been injured and the only thing that has kept 
this from becoming a more significant injury is luck. That thought process drives the first aid focus. Near miss 
reporting and investigation is always encouraged through the normal incident reporting system, and through Near 
Miss Reporting Cards that anonymously report near misses for trending. 
 
2.6.2 Medical Aids 
A team leader accompanies a injured worker to medical aid and must remain with the worker until they are 
released. All medical aids require a conference that involves the most senior site management and involvement 
from the person involved and their team leader. This formal inquiry is to determine causes and ensure corrective 
action is firmly in place. There is a time requirement for all medical aid conferences to occur within the time or 
injury. Typically this time period is within 8 hours of the incident. 
 
2.6.3 Lost Time Incident Reporting Process 
A process for how all incidents are reported, responded to, and reviewed after.  
 
2.7 Metric Definitions 
 
The following definitions are provided from a global perspective. Due to the various regulatory agencies around 
the world that have various reporting requirements, all definitions are provided to assist the reader in gaining a 
better understanding of the metrics involved in a well managed safety program. A mine site will have to select 
those metrics that are relevant to that region of the world. 
 
Fatal Injury (F) – An injury that results in death. 
 
Lost Time Injury (LTI) – An injury that results in absence from work. The length of absence required to qualify for 
an LTI varies from country to country (eg Australia one day, UK three days, South Africa 14 Days)  
 
Severe Injury (SI) – An injury that results in a minimum of two weeks off work. This definition is used by South 
Africa as the lost time injury measurement. 
 
Fatality Incidence Rate (FIR) – The number of fatalities per set number of employees. This is per 1,000 
employees in Australia (sometimes per 100,000 employees is also used). In the US it can refer to fatalities per 
200,000 hours worked. 
 
Disabling Injury Frequency Rate (DIFR) – The number of disabling injuries per 200,000 hours worked 
(US/Canada) or one million hours worked (rest of world). Includes all disabling injuries, whether or not they 
caused employees to be off work.  
 
Fatal Injury Frequency Rate (FIFR) – The number of fatal injuries per hours worked, normally one per million 
hours.

Lost Time Injury Frequency Rate (LTIFR) – The number of lost time injuries per 200,000 hours worked 
(US/Canada) or one million hours worked (rest of world) – again comparability depends on the LTI definition. 
Replaced in some country statistics by DIFR. 
 
Duration Rate (DR) – The average time lost for every lost time injury. This is a measure of the severity of the 
injuries occurring. Calculated by dividing the total number of days lost by the number of lost time injuries.  
 
Severity Rate (SR) – Calendar days lost per 200,000 hours worked (US/Canada) or one million hours worked 
(rest of world). 
 
No Days Lost (NDL) – Used in US, and refers to all injuries not resulting in a lost work day.  
 
Non Fatal Days Lost (NFDL) – Used in US, and refers to the number of days lost from non fatal injuries. 
 
Reportable/recordable Injury – Used in the US, and includes all incidents that require medical treatment, or 
result in death, or loss of consciousness, or inability to perform all job duties on any workday after the injury, or 
temporary assignment or transfer to another job Injuries involving “first aid only” are not reportable. Other 
countries define it similarly.  
 
NFDL incidence rate: Use in the US, and is based on NFDL injuries per 200,000 hours worked, MSHA replaced 
this with an all injury incidence rate in 2003. 
 
All injury incidence rate: Used in the US, and combines disabling injuries, medical – aid injuries and fatalities 
per 200,000 hours worked. Similar to reportable/recordable frequency rate.
