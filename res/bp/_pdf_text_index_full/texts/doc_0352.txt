1.0 Introduction  
 
Maintenance and Repair Contracts (MARC) can be both a source of revenue for a dealership 
and at the same time provide a high level of customer satisfaction. For both of these goals to be 
reached, it’s critical that a dealership uses a cross-functional approach that ensures the input 
and participation of multiple departments. 
 
Areas such as parts, service, sales, and administration need to be involved in developing the 
MARC rate. Each area can have critical assumptions that should be involved in rate 
development.  
 
The Dealer MARC Rate Development Best Practice ensures an enterprise communication of 
roles, responsibilities, and expectations. With these established, the goals of revenue 
generation and customer satisfaction are easier to reach. 
 
2.0 Best Practice Description 
 
2.1 Evaluate and Understand Customer Needs 
 
A dealer must thoroughly understand the customer’s requirements before establishing the per-
hour MARC rate. Those requirements must be documented and communicated and become the 
“inclusions” and “exclusions” in the per-hour rate. 
 
Among other things, the dealership gathers market intelligence about customer needs, industry 
trends, political and environmental conditions, and develops an understanding of competitive 
offerings in the service and parts after-market business. 
 
2.1.1 Inputs 
• Dealer/Caterpillar definition of product and market  
• Product and market strategies 
• Customer contact and interviews 
• External research/competition 
• Dealer solution history 
• Customer fingerprint on current business dealings (i.e. Parts / labor sales) 
• Contracts performance 
• Special pricing arrangements 
• Environmental and political conditions 
• Equipment sales 
• Economic forecast 
 
2.1.2 Steps 
1. View business from customers’ perspective 
2. Develop list of qualifying and determining needs based on customers’ behaviors 
3. Force rank all needs in order of importance 
4. Capture field data for competitor market penetration and performance  
5. Determine competitor capabilities, both current and future 
6. Review solution history

2.2 Complete Dealer Capability Review 
 
MARC rate development begins with a required dealer capability review. It compares current 
dealership capabilities to the customer request for service in the MARC, and documents the 
gaps. It is important to review and understand exactly what the customer wants and to 
determine if the dealer can deliver the request. This process needs to be completed up front in 
order to ensure sufficient time to explore a cost-benefit analysis of the opportunities presented 
by the customer, or to secure a supplier commitment for the service.  
 
The Dealer Capability Review is broken down into 4 section: 
1. General Contract Information 
2. Dealer Workshop Capability 
3. Dealer Component Repair and Salvage Capability 
4. Dealer Labour Skills Capability 
 
(See attached Dealer Capability Review Form.doc)  
 
 
2.3 Complete Due Diligence Process 
The due diligence process will be the safe source document to capture contract details, critical 
assumptions, site profile and capabilities, application information, Maintenance and Repair 
Process responsibilities and component risks. This work is done up front to ensure consistent 
contract communication across the dealer departments. 
 
For assistance with the Due Diligence process, please contact the CGM Regional MARC 
Consultant for your region.

2.5 Localize the Builder File 
 
Assembling the MARC Team is an important part of “Localizing” the Builder file. It is an 
important step to ensure the proposed site operations group can execute the MARC for the 
proposed parts and labor dollars for each repair option: R&I, Exchange Components, Running 
Repairs, and Preventive Maintenance (PM’s). 
 
The perspective must be from whatever facility will be used to deliver the service. Quoted PM 
times must be in line with the dealer’s capability at the Site. For example, if the quoted PM time 
is 25 hours for a 500-hour truck PM, can it be completed in 25 hours? Site-specific details (does 
the work need to be done outside, etc.) must be considered. If operations cannot execute a 
service in the hours listed in Builder, then adjustments MUST be made prior to tendering the 
rate per hour to the customer. 
 
For each Repair Option, compare the proposed intervals with the due diligence document and 
the territory experience for each model. Make adjustments as required to ensure the total 
number of repair events is calculated for the requested term.

2.6 Review Infrastructure Cost to Deliver Required Services 
 
Once repair options have been priced in accordance to the pricing strategy, additional 
infrastructure costs must be calculated. This includes such factors as: 
• Additional tools, equipment, facilities 
• Employees or skills required 
• Parts capabilities 
• Location 
• Freight 
• Storage 
• Contract administration 
• Service technicians and classifications 
• Management staff 
• Logistics (travel time, mileage, housing, meals) 
• Communication equipment 
• Computer systems.  
 
The infrastructure review may also reveal the customer requires a service on-site on a product 
currently not supported by the dealership. This process may require additional training to 
current staff or it may require that another resource with the specific skills be hired by the 
dealership. These additional infrastructure costs must be included in the final MARC contract.  
The attached worksheet template can be used to calculate the additional infrastructure costs 
required to execute the MARC. 
 
(See attached Infrastructure Requirements Form.xls)  
 
 
 
2.7 Identify Logistics Costs to Deliver the Required Services 
 
It is essential that any cost related to transporting tools, equipment or staff be accounted for in 
the final MARC cost model. The dealership can determine if a favorable cost-benefit business 
opportunity exists. The dealer must determine how to recoup all or part of the investment 
needed to meet those logistic requirements. 
 
(See attached Logistics Cost Worksheet.xls)

2.8 Identify Financial Costs to Deliver the Required Services 
 
Each MARC contract will be unique and will require an analysis of additional financial costs and 
risks the dealership must realize to deliver the proposed services. Financial costs include 
depreciation and additional insurance expenses, additional tax implications, the cost of money 
and other liabilities the dealership must identify prior to committing an obligation to deliver a 
MARC.  A worksheet template can be used to calculate the additional financial costs required to 
execute the MARC. 
 
(See attached Financial Costs Form.doc)  
 
 
2.9 Document Risk Decisions – Probability & Reserve 
 
Contract risk is documented as part of the due diligence process; however it is very important to 
calculate the “cost” of risk. Risk is defined in several areas of the contract; 
 
2.9.1 Before and After Failure Probability (80/20) 
 
The probability review entails accounting for repair event risk. Some components will “cost” 
slightly more than the repair option in the business plan, and some will “cost” slightly less. The 
80/20 probability process takes 80% of the before-failure repair option price and 20% of the 
after-failure repair option price, and combines both figures to establish the repair option price.   
 
A “what if” analysis can be performed using “80/20”, “70/30”, or “50/50”. The level of confidence 
the dealer has in the components will dictate the probability percentage used.  
 
The following example illustrates an engine repair using 80/20 for the first, second and third 
overhaul, and the remove and install labor to establish a rate per hour for the engine repair 
option.

2.9.2 Repair Reserve Contingency  
 
“Contingency” is one of the most difficult concepts in CALCULATOR to understand. It is also 
often referred to as “Repair Reserve”.  “Contingency” is used for two primary reasons: 
• To build risk into a contract. 
• To provide a means for having consistent pricing between an initial MARC sale and 
subsequent renewals of a contract. 
 
CALCULATOR is the software application used to establish a MARC rate for any piece of 
equipment. CALCULATOR interfaces with BUILDER for repair option information (labor hours, 
downtime, parts pricing and quantity, interval, and miscellaneous costs). This information is 
used over a specified contract term to produce a MARC rate. 
  
 
A simple example of “Contingency” is as follows: 
 
• Dealer prices a MARC contract on an engine that covers only a major overhaul. The major 
overhaul is scheduled to occur every 24,000 hours at a cost of $100,000. The contract term 
is 40,000 hours.

“Contingency” is defined as the cost of a component that is associated with the percentage of 
life used while under contract.  “Contingency” is only calculated for components that are to be 
installed during the contract term, but will be removed after the contract has expired. 
 
In the above scenario, the:  
• % of Life Used Under the Contract = 16,000/24,000 = 66% 
• % of Life Used not under the Contract = 8,000/24,000 = 33% 
 
In the above scenario, the “Contingency” = 66% of the cost of the component ($100,000) = 
$66,666 
 
Therefore, “Contingency” = (% of Life Used Under the Contract) * (Cost of Event) 
 
If “Contingency” were applied to this contract, the cost of the contract would be the sum of the 
first occurrence of the event under contract at 24,000 hours plus the “Contingency” amount of 
the second event. 
 
First Event    $100,000 
“Contingency”   $  66,666 
Total Cost  $166,666 
 
This scenario can vary depending on the frequency of the event and the term of the contract.

If, in the above example, the Trigger were set to 70% for this event, “Contingency” would not 
be calculated and the contract would be priced at $100,000. If the Trigger were set to 60% for 
this event, “Contingency” would be calculated and the contract would be priced at $166,666. 
 
Reserve (%) 
 
Since the Trigger causes an “all or nothing” scenario, an additional adjustment can be made 
to the “Contingency” formula using a Reserve percentage.  
 
Refer to the original example: 
 
Scenario 1: Trigger = 60% and Used Life = 66% - “Contingency” is calculated as $66,666 
Scenario 2: Trigger = 70% and Used Life = 66% - “Contingency” is calculated as $0 
 
Suppose in Scenario 2; there were a preference to calculate some “Contingency” (instead of 
$0).  
 
If the “Reserve %” were set to 50%, the above example would calculate Contingency as 
follows: 
1. Calculate the cost of Contingency, 
2. Apply the Trigger of 70%, 
3. The Used Life of the event (66%) is below the trigger %, 
4. In this case, the Reserve is calculated as 50% of the total “Contingency” or $33,333 
5. Total cost for the contract = $133,333.

If the Dealer creates a MARC using “Before Failure’ and “After Failure” to provide for risk, the 
user should be careful when applying “Contingency” to avoid jeopardizing the 
competitiveness of the rate. 
 
Contingency for Smoothing Contract Extensions 
• In the example, the dealer calculates a 40,000-hour contract for $100,000. The cost-per-
hour (CPH) is $2.50 
• If an extension is offered after the end of the initial contract for 8,000 hours. The CPH 
would be $12.50 
• The reason is: the customer has a shorter time frame (8,000 vs. 40,000 hours) to pay for 
the Engine. 
• If contingency is applied to the first contract. The total is $166,666, and the CPH would be 
$4.17 
• On the extension the customer prepays for $66,666 of the Engine in the first contract. The 
total of the extension is $33,333. The CPH is $4.17 
 
2.9.3 Component Intervals  
 
Consider the number of rebuilds over the MARC term. Selecting appropriate component 
intervals is an important element of the rate development process.  When selecting appropriate 
component intervals, consider the number of components required as well as how realistic and 
achievable the interval is.   
 
Small incremental improvements in component life can have a significant impact on the total 
contract cost.  Average Component Lives can impact the total number of components over the 
contract term.

2.10 Develop Rate Per Hour using Calculator 
 
Once the due diligence is complete and the infrastructure requirements have been reviewed, 
Calculator can be used to combine repair options and infrastructure in establishing the “rate-per-
hour,” to present to the customer.  
 
Calculator contains a complete listing of Repair Options, PM’s, Infrastructure, Travel Time & 
Mileage, and Management Fees that equate to a rate-per-hour.

3.0 Implementation Steps 
 
Each of the steps explained above. 
 
1. Evaluate and understand customer needs 
2. Dealer Capability Review 
3. Complete due diligence process 
4. CliPP Analysis 
5. CGM Builder Centerline file (refer to CGM Builder Best Practice)

7. Review Infrastructure costs to deliver required services 
8. Identify logistics costs to deliver required services

4.0 Benefits 
 
• Predictable revenue stream to dealer 
• Enterprise approach to the Mining MARC opportunity.  
• Employee satisfaction will result from participating in the rate development process and clearly 
communicated goals and expectations.  
• Customer satisfaction resulting from proper dealer execution and communication. Ensuring 
MARC team members understand their roles and expectations, execution is a simply a 
function of extending authority and allowing the staff to be responsible for their own areas. 
 
 
5.0 Resources Required 
 
• The primary resource required to implement the Rate Development Process is an enterprise 
approach to MARC management. It is imperative to approach the MARC management 
business from top management down. Top-down directives ensure the staff is empowered to 
make business decisions and has the authority and management support required to be 
successful.  
 
• Each dealer department will need a dedicated resource as a representative to support the 
project sponsor and MARC manager when implementing an enterprise Rate Development 
Process. 
 
• Builder and Calculator software.
