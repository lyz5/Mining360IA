1.0 Introduction  
There are two acceptable accounting options to record transactions for maintenance and repair 
contracts.  The first option is accrual accounting (deferred accounts), which is considered the best 
practice for recognizing and recording transactions related to maintenance and repair contracts.  
This method allows the measurement of not only the current cash in flows / outflows but also 
allows the inclusion of future expected cash inflows / outflows.  The main focus of accrual 
accounting is the proper matching of revenues to expenses.   The second option called cash 
accounting only provides a snapshot of past financial activity up to today.  This method does not 
match revenues to costs.  The accrual accounting method is usually the required accounting 
process for companies over a certain size in most countries. There are exceptions to this rule. 
Some geographic locations still adhere to the cash basis of accounting for legal entity reporting.  
This best practice is not intended to replace the accounting process used by an entity but to give 
a better understanding why accrual accounting will give a more accurate picture of contract 
performance.  
 
2.0 Best Practice Description 
This best practice is intended as guidance to help contract managers to understand the key 
concepts of accrual accounting vs. cash basis accounting and the benefits of accrual accounting 
to effectively measure contract profitability.  
 
3.0 Implementation Steps 
 
Understanding Cash vs. Accrual Accounting Practices. 
   
Cash Basis Accounting: 
Revenues are recognized & recorded when cash is actually received. 
Expenses are recognized & recorded when they actually are paid. 
 
Accrual Basis Accounting: 
Revenues are recognized and recorded when it is earned. 
Expenses are recognized and recorded when they are incurred. 
 
Accounting for Separately Priced Extended Warranty and Product Support Maintenance 
Contracts.  FASB Technical Bulletin #90-1. 
 
Revenues: Revenues from product support maintenance contracts should be deferred and 
recognized as income on a straight-line basis over the contract period except in those 
circumstances in which sufficient historical evidence indicates that the costs of performing 
services under contract are incurred on other than a straight-line basis.  In those circumstances, 
revenue should be recognized over the contract period in proportion to the expected costs to be 
incurred in performing services under the contract.

Immediate Revenue Recognition:  Product maintenance contracts are not incidental to the sale 
of a product but are independent transactions under which the customer agrees to purchase at a 
determined rate and the provider of services agrees to perform certain services at that rate. The 
customer decision to purchase Caterpillar equipment cannot entail any obligation to purchase the 
maintenance & repair contract, nor oblige the dealer to provide any additional services.  The 
dealer may lower product prices in expectation of selling maintenance contracts. However it is the 
dealers pricing or marketing strategy, that determines the appropriate financial reporting of 
transactions.  Because the obligation extends over a period of time, immediate revenue 
recognition is inappropriate. 
 
Costs: Costs that are directly related to the acquisition of a contract and that would not have 
been incurred but for the acquisition of that contract should be deferred and charged to expense 
in proportion to the revenue recognized.  All other costs, such as costs of services performed 
under the contract, general and administrative expenses should be charged to expense as 
incurred. 
 
Loss: A loss should be recognized on product maintenance agreements if the sum of the 
expected costs of providing services under the contracts exceeds related unearned revenue.  
Product maintenance contracts should be grouped in a consistent manner to determine if a loss 
exists.  If a loss exists then a liability should be recognized for the loss. 
 
Revenue Recognition Options: When applying FTB 90-1, the contract manager should always 
evaluate the expected pattern of maintenance related costs and should not default to recognizing 
revenue on a straight line basis because of lack of “sufficient historical evidence” regarding such 
costs.  Although the evaluation of the expected pattern of maintenance-related costs should focus 
on future related cash outflows, historical evidence about past costs, if available, should be used 
to assist in that evaluation.  It the contract manager does not have sufficient historical evidence 
on which to base an estimate of future maintenance costs, such information should be available 
from other sources. In those circumstances, revenues should be recognized over the contract in 
proportion to the expected costs to be incurred in performing services under the contract. 
 
  
Revenue Recognition Criteria:  Revenues must be recognized under accrual accounting when 
the following four criteria are met. 
 
Persuasive evidence of an arrangement exists. 
Delivery has occurred or services have been rendered. 
The seller’s price to the buyer is fixed or determinable. 
Collection is reasonably assured.

Requirements for Effective Accrual Processes: 
  
Managers must understand the operational processes. 
Create standard jobs. 
Then develop life cycle schedule from standard jobs. 
Create a budget. 
Practice proactive event scheduling. 
Condition monitoring. 
Understand the sold solution. 
Accountability – roles and responsibility. 
Understand variance analysis. 
 
 
4.0 Benefits 
 
Benefits of Matching Revenues to Costs: 
 
Provide accurate feedback to management on contract performance. 
Have the ability to timely identify non-profitable contracts so corrective action can take place. 
Provide accurate pricing for future contract negotiations. 
Let the accurate and true numbers drive operational decisions 
Focus efficiency improvements where they belong. 
Focus training on where it belongs. 
Focus investments where they belong.
